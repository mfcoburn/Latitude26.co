sections:
  - heading: Practice philosophy
    body: |
      Three convictions shape how this practice works.

      **Time is a clinical instrument, not a courtesy.**

      Most diagnostic misses are not failures of knowledge. They are failures of
      attention: the symptom not mentioned because there was no room to mention
      it, the follow-up question never asked, the pattern invisible because
      nobody had time to look for it. Ninety minutes is not hospitality. It is
      how the medicine gets done properly.

      **Continuity compounds.**

      A provider who has known you for six years is working with something no
      first visit can supply: the shape of your normal. Knowing what is
      unremarkable *for you* is what makes a change visible while it is still
      small. That knowledge accumulates, and it cannot be transferred,
      delegated, or reconstructed from a chart.

      **The relationship should answer to you and to no one else.**

      When a practice bills insurance, a third party with its own interests sits
      inside every clinical decision. When members pay the practice directly,
      that seat is empty. There is no incentive to see more people, order more
      tests, or schedule a follow-up your care does not require. The practice is
      paid to look after you well enough that you stay.
  - heading: Founding story
    body: |
      We live in a hectic world with high demands on our time. More often than
      not, the thing that gets pushed to the bottom of the list is our own
      health.

      Medicine used to be about treating and nurturing patients, about
      optimizing someone's health and well-being over the long run. Somewhere
      along the way, that got lost in automated phone trees, fifteen-minute
      appointment slots, insurance red tape, and the shareholder bottom line.

      When I was active duty in the military, I didn't realize how fortunate I
      was. There was no minimum number of patients I had to see in a day. I was
      paid the same whether I saw twenty patients or three. I had, essentially,
      as much time as I needed to address every one of my patients' concerns. So
      I gave people the time and attention they deserved, and they felt it. They
      felt seen. They felt cared for. And my clinical outcomes reflected it.

      When I retired from the military, I discovered how difficult that kind of
      care is to find on the outside. I struggle to get it myself. And I'm a
      medical provider. If someone who knows this system from the inside has to
      fight for it, imagine what it takes for everyone standing outside of it.

      I am now at a point in my career where I can build the practice I would
      want for my own family: one where you are never rushed, never a number, and
      never left to figure it out alone. That is exactly what Latitude 26 is.

      **Colleen B. Coburn, PA-C**
      *Founding Provider*
