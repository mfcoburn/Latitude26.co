As the year draws to a close, the calendar can fill with gatherings, observances, travel, and family traditions. Whether you celebrate Christmas, Hanukkah, Winter Solstice, Kwanzaa, or another meaningful occasion, this season can bring both connection and added demands.

You may be looking forward to familiar rituals while wondering how you'll manage everything. You may be observing several traditions, creating new ones, or choosing not to celebrate at all.

Whatever this season means to you, your needs deserve a place in it.

For some, this time of year brings welcome company. For others, it brings loneliness, financial pressure, difficult relationships, or reminders of someone deeply missed. Many people experience several of these feelings at once.

Our prior journal entry explored caring for your whole self and speaking up when you don't feel like yourself. During the winter holidays, those ideas become especially personal. How do you make room for your own needs when so much seems to need your attention?

## Recognize what feels demanding

Seasonal stress does not look the same for everyone.

You might feel responsible for keeping traditions going, preparing special meals, hosting family, or making celebrations meaningful for children or grandchildren. You may be balancing religious or cultural observances with work, caregiving, or your own health needs.

In a household with different traditions, you may be navigating several calendars and expectations. You might also feel overlooked when the celebrations around you don't reflect your beliefs or experiences.

Perhaps the challenge is an empty calendar rather than a crowded one. A quiet home, distance from loved ones, or a change in family circumstances can make this season feel particularly difficult.

Pause and ask: What feels hardest about this time of year for me?

An honest answer can help you recognize where you need support, what you want to keep, and what you might approach differently.

## Decide what matters most to you

It can be easy to follow a seasonal schedule without ever choosing it. You do something because you have always done it, because someone expects it, or because saying no feels uncomfortable.

Give yourself permission to consider what's realistic now.

Which traditions feel meaningful? Which gatherings do you enjoy? Are there observances for which you want to protect time? What can your energy, schedule, and budget comfortably accommodate?

Perhaps this year calls for a smaller meal, fewer gifts, a shorter visit, or help with preparations. You might choose to preserve a cherished ritual while simplifying the plans around it.

If you don't celebrate, consider what you want from these weeks. Is it companionship, rest, time outdoors, the comfort of your usual routine or something else entirely?

Your plans can reflect your values and present circumstances.

## Use clear, kind boundaries

A boundary can be a simple explanation of what you can offer or what you need.

“We would love to join you for dinner, but we'll need to leave early.”

“I’m keeping spending simple this year.”

“I can bring a dish, but I’m unable to host.”

“That evening is important to our family’s observance. Could we choose another date?”

“I need a quiet evening, so I’ll sit this one out.”

You can appreciate an invitation while making a choice that respects your needs. A brief, considerate answer is often enough.

If you share responsibilities with others, be specific about the help you need. Asking someone to handle shopping, transportation, or cleanup gives them a clear way to contribute.

## Leave some breathing room in the day

During a busy season, ordinary routines can become harder to protect. Meals get squeezed between errands. Evenings run later. Time alone or with someone you trust may disappear from the calendar altogether.

Look for a few familiar moments you would like to preserve. It could be sitting down for breakfast, taking a comfortable walk, reading before bed, or having an unhurried conversation.

Leave space between commitments where you can. Having a little time to get home, settle in, or prepare for the next day may make your schedule feel more manageable.

These moments deserve a place on the calendar alongside the plans you make for everyone else.

## Allow the season to feel different

Grief, separation, illness, and changing relationships can reshape this time of year. A tradition that once felt comforting may feel painful. You may want company one day and privacy the next.

You can tell someone you trust what would feel helpful. Perhaps you'd like to share a memory, prepare a meaningful family dish, or step away from a familiar gathering for now.

If you are supporting someone else, ask what this season means to them. Offer a specific invitation without pressure: “Would you like some company this weekend?” Respect their answer and leave room for it to change.

Joy, sadness, gratitude, and disappointment may all have a place. You don't have to match the mood of the celebrations around you.

## Keep your health in the conversation

Pay attention to how you're doing as the season unfolds. If you notice persistent or concerning changes in how you feel physically or emotionally, bring them to your healthcare provider.

You can begin simply: “This time of year has been difficult, and I haven’t been feeling like myself.”

Share what's changed, how it is affecting your day, and what concerns you. You do not need to know the cause before asking for support.

If travel or observances affect your availability for appointments, discuss those plans with your care team so you can prepare together.

## Approach the coming year with room to adjust

As the calendar turns, you may feel pressure to make sweeping changes or set ambitious goals, whether or not January marks a meaningful new beginning for you.

You can choose one practical commitment that reflects what you need such as making an appointment you have postponed, asking for help, protecting time for a meal, or reconnecting with someone whose company you enjoy.

Choose something that fits your life, with room to adjust as circumstances change.

However you spend the winter holidays, include yourself in the care and consideration you offer others.

What would make this season feel a little more manageable for you?
